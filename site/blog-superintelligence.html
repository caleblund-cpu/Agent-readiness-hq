First it was AI. Then AGI. Now the word is superintelligence.

October 15, 2026

Every few years, the technology industry renames its destination. For a decade, the stated goal was AGI, artificial general intelligence: a system that could match human performance across most cognitive work. Lately, a different word has been displacing it. When Meta reorganized its AI efforts in 2025, it did not call the new division AGI Labs. It called it Superintelligence Labs, and it has used the word superintelligence plainly ever since, to mean AI that could exceed human ability not at one narrow task, but broadly. Other labs have started using the term too. The vocabulary is shifting again, and vocabulary usually shifts first.

The industry has renamed this goal before

This pattern is not new. The field itself began with a rename: in 1956, a workshop at Dartmouth deliberately coined "artificial intelligence" as a fresh label for work that had previously gone by clunkier names. Since then the vocabulary has rotated through machine learning, deep learning, and generative AI, and each rotation tended to mark two things at once. There was usually a genuine capability jump underneath, and there was also a marketing reset on top of it, separating the exciting present from whatever had disappointed people last cycle. AGI itself rose to prominence partly because plain "AI" had come to mean chatbots and photo filters to most people. Superintelligence looks like the next turn of the same wheel.

What the new word is trying to say

AGI was always an awkward term. Nobody could quite agree on where the line sat, and every demo invited the same argument about whether this counted as general intelligence yet. Superintelligence reaches back to an older definition. In his 2014 book Superintelligence, the philosopher Nick Bostrom defined it as an intellect that greatly exceeds human cognitive performance in virtually all domains of interest. For years the word belonged mostly to academics and essayists. Now it is on lab names and recruiting pages.

Part of that is ambition signaling. If AGI has started to sound close, or at least contested, moving the label keeps the goal suitably grand. Part of it may be a more honest statement of the target: the largest labs are no longer describing parity with humans as the finish line. They are describing something past it.

A rename is marketing, and also a tell

A new word does not change what the technology can do this quarter, and it is worth treating any label as a signal about direction rather than a delivery date. But labels are not nothing. Companies tend to adopt the vocabulary of the future they intend to spend money building, and when the biggest labs converge on a term, it can potentially shape hiring, headlines, and eventually the words customers use without thinking about it. It is possible that superintelligence becomes the everyday word, the way AI did. It might just as easily fade, the way earlier buzzwords faded. Either way, the trend underneath the label has been steadier than any of the labels.

The part that does not depend on the name

Whatever wins the naming contest, the systems actually arriving in the meantime are narrower and more practical: AI agents. These are tools that research options, compare providers, summarize what they find, and increasingly complete tasks on behalf of the person who sent them. Your customers may never say the word superintelligence out loud. They are already asking assistants to find a contractor, gather quotes, and explain the differences.

That is where the vocabulary meets a local business. An agent can only work with what it can read. If it can find your services, hours, service area, and contact details in a form it can verify, your business could be part of the answer it puts together. If those facts are buried inside images, tangled in scripts, or inconsistent from page to page, the agent will likely move on to a business it could understand. No announcement is made. You simply were not in the answer.

People first, machines second

None of this changes what a website is for. Its first job is winning business from people. Being readable by machines comes second, and mostly it means stating clearly what is already true about your business, in places a machine can reach. That work looks the same under every label the industry has used so far, which is a good reason not to chase the vocabulary.
