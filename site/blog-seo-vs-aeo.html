SEO vs AEO: What Changed About How Customers Find You

October 21, 2026 · By Agent Readiness HQ

For twenty years, getting found online meant one thing: showing up in Google's list of links. That discipline has a name, SEO, and most local businesses have at least a working sense of it. Keywords, reviews, a listing that stays current.

Now a second way of being found is growing up alongside the first, and it comes with its own alphabet: AEO, GEO. If a marketing pitch has used one of those terms at you lately, this post is the plain-English version of what they mean, and what they leave out.

SEO: get found by search engines

SEO is Search Engine Optimization. A customer types "plumber near me," a search engine returns a list, and the businesses on that list get the clicks. SEO is about ranking well in that list: clear pages, accurate listings, reviews, and a site that loads properly.

That has not gone away. Search engines still send enormous numbers of customers to local businesses, and the basics still matter.

AEO: get quoted by answer engines

AEO is Answer Engine Optimization. Its twin term is GEO, Generative Engine Optimization, and the two are used almost interchangeably.

The difference from SEO is the customer behavior underneath it. Instead of typing a search and clicking through a list of links, more people now ask an AI assistant a direct question: "Who is a good plumber near me that handles water heaters?" The assistant does not hand back ten links. It gives an answer, and it may name a few businesses inside that answer.

That answer has to come from somewhere. The assistant builds it from the public information it can find about each business: website pages, listings, reviews, and whatever else is stated clearly enough to rely on. When the facts are easy to find and agree with each other, the assistant can describe a business with more confidence. When they are scattered or contradictory, it may leave the business out, or describe it in a way the owner would not recognize.

AEO is the work of being one of the businesses an answer like that could draw on. Clear, consistent, well-structured information potentially helps an AI system describe a business accurately when it answers. There are no guarantees here, and anyone who promises a citation or a ranking in AI answers is promising something nobody controls.

The layer both of them skip: being understood by an agent

Here is the part neither SEO nor AEO fully covers.

When an AI assistant looks into a local business, an agent often visits the website itself and tries to read it the way a careful researcher would, only much faster and much more literally. It looks for the basics: what services are offered, where, at what prices, during which hours, and how to make contact. Increasingly, it may also try to act on what it finds, comparing options side by side or starting a booking on the customer's behalf.

A website can look great to a person and still be hard for an agent to read. Services might live only inside images. Prices might be stated three different ways on three different pages, or not stated at all. The file that tells automated visitors which pages matter might be missing. None of that stops a human customer who can call and ask. It could slow down or mislead an automated one, which has no way to ask a follow-up question.

That layer is what we call agent readiness: whether an agent can access a website, read it, and come away with an accurate picture of the business. SEO gets a business found by search engines. AEO aims at being quoted by answer engines. Agent readiness is about being understood correctly in the first place, which is the step the other two quietly assume has already happened. (We have written separately about the agent economy behind this shift, and why it is growing.)

This is not a website redesign. The introduction file is a supplement, not a substitute. It cannot make up for unclear pages, blocked access, missing structured data, or facts that are wrong in the first place.

What this means for a local business

Three practical takeaways:

  • Keep doing the SEO basics. Accurate listings, current hours, and real reviews possibly matter as much as they ever did, because AI systems draw on the same public information.
  • Treat clarity as an asset. Services, service area, hours, and pricing stated plainly in text an agent can read are easier for any system, human or automated, to get right.
  • Check the machine layer once. Most owners have never seen their website the way an agent sees it. A quick check can show whether the basics are in place or whether an agent is likely working from an incomplete picture.
